Fundamental Forces: Electrostatic vs Gravitational Force

Aspect Electrostatic Force (Coulomb) Gravitational Force (Newton)
Interaction Nature Both attractive (unlike charges) and repulsive (like charges) Strictly attractive only (mass is always positive)

Step-by-Step Derivation: Electric Potential Due to an Isolated Point Charge

Step 1: Work Definition in Electric Field

Electric potential V at distance r is the external work required to bring a unit positive test charge (+q₀) from infinity to r without acceleration: V = - ∫ [∞ to r] E · dr.

Step 2: Express Radial Field Intensity

The electric field of charge Q is directed radially outward: E(x) = (1 / 4πε₀) · (Q / x²).

Step 3: Evaluate the Definite Integral

W = - ∫ [∞ to r] (k·Q / x²) dx = - k·Q · [-1/x] [∞ to r] = k·Q · (1/r - 1/∞) = k·Q / r.

Step 4: Final Potential Expression

V = (1 / 4πε₀) · (Q / r). Electric potential falls off as 1/r, whereas electric field falls off as 1/r².

Worked Example

Two small conducting spheres placed in air at a distance of 30 cm carry charges of 2 × 10⁻⁷ C and 3 × 10⁻⁷ C respectively. What is the electrostatic force between them?

  1. Identify Parameters q₁ = 2 × 10⁻⁷ C, q₂ = 3 × 10⁻⁷ C, r = 30 cm = 0.3 m, k = 9 × 10⁹ N·m²/C².
  2. Apply Coulomb's Formula F = k · (q₁ · q₂) / r² = (9 × 10⁹) · (2 × 10⁻⁷ · 3 × 10⁻⁷) / (0.3)²
  3. Calculate Resultant F = (9 × 6 × 10⁻⁵) / (0.09) = 54 × 10⁻⁵ / 0.09 = 6 × 10⁻³ N = 0.006 N.

Answer: F = 6 × 10⁻³ N (repulsive, since both charges are positive).

If the distance between two stationary point charges is halved, by what factor does the electrostatic force between them change?

Why?

According to Coulomb's Law F ∝ 1/r². Halving r to r/2 gives 1/(r/2)² = 4/r², quadrupling the force.
